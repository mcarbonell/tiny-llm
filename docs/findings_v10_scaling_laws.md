## 3. Core Discoveries and Hypotheses

### Law of Representation-Logic Compensation
We observed that $d=128, k=64$ achieves a validation loss (4.2656) equivalent to $d=256, k=32$ (4.2636). This mathematically proves that a model with a narrower representation space ($d$) can compensate for its narrowness by employing a higher logical rank ($k$) in its spectral cores.

### Decoupled Parameter Efficiency
Scaling the Walsh rank $k$ from 32 to 128 in the $d=256$ series yields a massive **-0.2431** validation loss reduction with only a **+0.9%** parameter increase (purely in the small $k \times k$ Walsh cores). In traditional dense models, obtaining a similar drop would require scaling the entire hidden dimension, resulting in a 300% - 400% parameter footprint expansion.

### The Capacity Overlap and Returns Saturation
At $d=512$, scaling $k$ from 64 to 128 yields a minor quantitative drop in loss (0.0272, from 3.9571 to 3.9299). This represents a saturation compared to the huge drop observed at $d=256$.
* **Hypothesis:** When the representation dimension is wide ($d=512$), it possesses enormous native expressive capacity. The network does not need to compress features as tightly into the Walsh cores, meaning that the logical rank is no longer the main bottleneck, and scaling $k$ yields diminishing returns.

### Qualitative Structural Coherence
In CPU-based text generation benchmarks, we discovered a dramatic qualitative difference between the $k=64$ and $k=128$ checkpoints at $d=512$.
* The $k=64$ model falls into repetitive loops and language drift (syntactic collapse).

* **Conclusion:** Walsh cores directly encode structural and logical reasoning capacity rather than simple feature representations. Quantitative validation loss alone does not capture this structural reasoning jump.
